Chapter One
What is Risk?
Before we can manage risk, we need to know what it actually is — and clear up the misconceptions that follow it everywhere.

1.1   Common Myths About Risk

Walk into any organisation and ask ten people what "risk" means. You'll likely hear: "something that goes wrong", or "that's the Risk Manager's job", or "we can't afford to think about risk, we're too busy."

These are myths — and clearing them out is the first step to building a real understanding of risk management.

❌
Myth: Risk = bad things that happen
Risk is uncertainty — not just negative outcomes. ISO 31000 explicitly recognises positive effects (opportunities) alongside threats.
❌
Myth: Risk management is the Risk Manager's job
Every employee in every role owns the risks created by their daily activities. Risk is a shared responsibility across all three lines of defence.
❌
Myth: Good risk management eliminates all risk
Impossible — and undesirable. The goal is taking the right risks at the right level to achieve your objectives. Eliminating risk means eliminating opportunity.
❌
Myth: Risk management is expensive
A basic risk identification checklist costs nothing. Research by PMI shows reactive risk management costs 3–5× more than proactive planning.
💡 Real World

Airbnb (2009): Letting strangers sleep in people's homes was a massive reputational and legal risk. Instead of avoiding it, they managed it through insurance, identity verification and reviews. Today Airbnb is worth over $75 billion. Eliminating that risk would have killed the business before it started.

1.2   The ISO 31000 Definition

ISO 31000:2018 is the international standard for risk management. It provides universal principles and guidelines that apply to any organisation, in any industry, anywhere in the world.

At its heart, the standard defines risk with just six words:

ISO 31000:2018 — Definition of Risk
"The effect of uncertainty on objectives"
Source: ISO 31000:2018, Section 3.1 — iso.org/standard/65694.html

Six words. But they contain three powerful concepts that are worth unpacking individually — because once you understand all three, the entire field of risk management starts to make sense.

1.3   Breaking It Down: Effect · Uncertainty · Objectives

Effect

An "effect" is a deviation from what was expected. It is not the risk itself — it is what would happen if the uncertain event occurs. Effects can be positive or negative.

📌 Example

A restaurant plans for 100 customers on Saturday. A heavy rainstorm (uncertainty) could reduce footfall to 40 — a negative effect on revenue. Or, if they set up indoor entertainment to take advantage of the weather, they may attract an extra 30 customers — a positive effect. Both are consequences of the same uncertainty.

Uncertainty

Uncertainty is the state of not fully knowing whether or how something will happen. It is not the same as ignorance — you can be aware of an uncertainty without knowing its outcome. ISO 31000 recognises three types:

TypeDescriptionExample
Known-KnownThings we know that we know. We can plan directly for them.Our annual software licence renews in March. We budget for it.
Known-UnknownThings we know exist, but we don't know the outcome. Most risk management focuses here.We know a regulatory review is coming — we don't know the findings.
Unknown-Unknown"Black Swan" territory. Things we don't know we don't know.No organisation modelled a global pandemic shutting all offices in 2020.
🔑 Key Principle

Risk management focuses most on Known-Unknowns — the uncertainties we can anticipate and prepare for. Black Swans, by definition, are the hardest to manage, but a strong risk culture reduces their impact significantly.

Objectives

Risk only exists in relation to what you are trying to achieve. Without an objective, there is no risk. A fire in an empty field is not a risk. A fire near your warehouse IS — because it threatens your operational objective of maintaining inventory and continuity.

Objectives exist at every level of an organisation:

  • Strategic objectives — long-term goals set by the Board and senior management
  • Operational objectives — department and project-level targets
  • Individual objectives — your personal KPIs, tasks and quality standards

1.4   Positive vs Negative Risk

One of the most common misconceptions is that risk is always bad. ISO 31000 is clear: risk includes both threats (negative effects) and opportunities (positive effects).

⚠
Threat (Negative Risk)
A key supplier goes bankrupt, disrupting your production line. A cyberattack exposes customer data. A flood damages your warehouse stock.
🚀
Opportunity (Positive Risk)
A competitor exits your market, opening new customer segments. A regulatory change favours your product line. A viral social media post doubles your enquiries overnight.
📌 Real-World Reference
Netflix vs Blockbuster — Strategic Risk and Opportunity
When Netflix approached Blockbuster in 2000 offering to sell for $50 million, Blockbuster declined. Netflix had identified streaming and subscription models as opportunities — Blockbuster saw only the disruption threat. By 2010, Blockbuster filed for bankruptcy. Netflix's willingness to embrace uncertainty turned into a $200 billion business.

Reference: BBC — The Blockbuster Story (bbc.com/news/business-42765580)

Chapter Two
Types of Risk
Risk comes in many forms. Recognising which type you're dealing with is the first step toward managing it effectively.

2.1   The 6 Core Risk Categories

While every organisation faces unique risks, most fall into six broadly recognised categories. Understanding these categories helps you structure conversations about risk, identify blind spots, and ensure nothing important is missed.

🏛
Strategic Risk
The risk that your organisation's strategy fails to deliver — due to wrong decisions, missed market trends, or being out-competed. These risks originate from or impact the organisation's long-term direction.
Classic case: Kodak invented the digital camera in 1975 but chose to suppress it to protect film revenue. By 2012, they filed for bankruptcy. Read the HBR analysis →
⚙
Operational Risk
The risk of loss from inadequate or failed internal processes, people, systems — or from external events. This is the most common risk category in day-to-day organisational life.
Classic case: In 2017, a British Airways engineer accidentally switched off a data centre power supply. 75,000 passengers were stranded. Cost: £80 million. BBC report →
💰
Financial Risk
The risk of financial loss through fraud, bad debt, poor cash flow management, currency fluctuations, or weak financial controls. Financial risk can be both a cause and a consequence of other risk types.
Classic case: JP Morgan lost $6.2 billion in 2012 due to a single trader's excessive positions — traced back to a copy-paste error in an Excel risk model. Full story →
📋
Compliance Risk
The risk of legal penalties, fines, or reputational damage from failing to comply with laws, regulations, internal policies or contractual obligations. In regulated industries, this is existential.
Classic case: In 2023, Meta was fined €1.2 billion by Irish regulators for violating GDPR — the largest GDPR fine ever issued at the time. BBC report →
🔐
Cyber Risk
The risk of financial loss, operational disruption or data damage from digital attacks — including ransomware, phishing, data breaches and insider threats. One of the fastest-growing risk categories globally.
Classic case: The 2021 Colonial Pipeline ransomware attack shut down fuel supply to 17 US states for 6 days. The company paid $4.4M ransom. Entry point: one compromised password with no MFA. Full story →
🌐
Reputational Risk
The risk that negative publicity, poor customer experience, ethical failures or association with controversy damages your brand and erodes stakeholder trust — often triggered by failures in other risk categories.
Classic case: In 2017, a video of a United Airlines passenger being forcibly removed went viral. Within 24 hours, United's market value dropped by $1.4 billion. BBC report →

2.2   How Risks Cascade

One of the most important insights in risk management is that risks rarely occur in isolation. A single event can ripple across multiple categories simultaneously — what we call the cascade effect.

📌 Case Study: Colonial Pipeline (2021)

One ransomware attack (Cyber Risk) → Systems went offline for 6 days (Operational Risk) → $4.4M ransom paid + massive revenue losses (Financial Risk) → Fuel shortages in 17 states caused panic buying (Reputational Risk) → Regulators launched investigations into pipeline security standards (Compliance Risk).

One event. Five risk categories. All triggered simultaneously.

Reference: CISA — What We Learned from the Colonial Pipeline Attack

This is why risk management cannot be done in silos. Organisations that manage each risk type separately, without seeing the interconnections, will always be caught off-guard when one event triggers a chain reaction.

Chapter Three
Initiating Risk Identification
Knowing what risk is means little if you can't find it. This chapter gives you the process and the tools to identify risks systematically and confidently.

3.1   The ISO 31000 Risk Identification Process

Risk identification is not about brainstorming a list of bad things. It is a structured process of systematically asking what could affect your objectives — positively or negatively — before it happens.

ISO 31000 defines a clear five-step approach to risk identification:

#StepWhat It MeansExample
1Understand ContextWhat are our objectives? Who are our stakeholders? What is our environment?A bank branch's objective is to serve customers securely within regulatory limits.
2Identify Risk EventsWhat events could prevent or help us achieve those objectives?Staff error on a transaction, system outage, competitor offering lower rates.
3Identify CausesWhat triggers each risk? Human error? Technology failure? External events?System outage caused by: vendor update failure, power cut, cyberattack.
4Identify ConsequencesIf this risk occurs — what happens? Financial loss? Regulatory fine?System outage → customers cannot transact → complaints → regulatory review.
5Document in Risk RegisterRecord every identified risk, assign an owner, set a review date.Risk ID: R-007 | Owner: IT Manager | Review: Monthly.
🔑 ISO 31000 Principle

Risk identification should be iterative and ongoing — not a one-off annual workshop. Risks change as your environment changes. The organisations with the best risk cultures treat risk identification as a continuous conversation, not a compliance checkbox.

3.2   Risk Identification Techniques

Different situations call for different approaches. Below are the most effective risk identification techniques used in professional practice — each with a clear explanation, its advantages, its disadvantages, and guidance on when to use it.

📌
Anonymous Sticky Notes
Anonymous Individual Input · Low Barrier
Participants write risks on sticky notes (physical or digital — e.g. Miro, MURAL) without putting their name on them. All notes are posted on a shared board and grouped by category or theme.
How to run it: Give everyone 5–10 minutes to write risks silently. Collect all notes. Group similar ones together. Discuss as a team — but without attributing any idea to a specific person. Tools: physical Post-it notes, or digital equivalents like Miro, MURAL, or Google Jamboard.
✓ Advantages
  • Removes fear of judgment — people share risks they'd never say aloud
  • Surfaces hidden organisational risks that senior staff may not see
  • Highly inclusive — everyone contributes equally regardless of seniority
  • Quick to run — even large groups can complete in 20–30 minutes
✗ Disadvantages
  • Risks noted without context — hard to understand without follow-up
  • Anonymous input can sometimes produce vague or irrelevant items
  • Doesn't naturally generate discussion around causes or consequences
  • Grouping and synthesis requires skilled facilitation
Best for: Teams where hierarchy or culture might prevent open discussion. Excellent as a first step before a more structured session.
🗣
Facilitated Group Discussions
Open Dialogue · Real-Time Depth
A facilitator leads a structured group conversation using guiding questions such as "What could prevent us from meeting our targets?" or "What has gone wrong before, and what almost went wrong?"
How to run it: Set a clear objective for the session. Use open questions to draw out risks. Capture all ideas without filtering. Encourage participants to build on each other's ideas. A skilled facilitator prevents dominant voices from shutting down others.
✓ Advantages
  • Ideas can be explored in real-time — causes and consequences emerge naturally
  • Collective intelligence: one person's comment often triggers another's insight
  • Builds shared understanding and team ownership of risks
  • Flexible — can be tailored to any team size or focus area
✗ Disadvantages
  • Dominant personalities can steer or suppress discussion
  • Groupthink risk — teams may converge on obvious risks only
  • Quality depends heavily on facilitator skill
  • Junior staff may stay silent in the presence of senior leadership
Best for: Cross-functional teams where diverse perspectives are important. Combine with anonymous sticky notes as a warm-up for maximum output.
🏛
Structured Risk Workshops
Deep Dive · Multi-Technique
A dedicated risk workshop is a planned, facilitated session — typically half a day or full day — specifically designed to identify, assess and prioritise risks for a project, department or process. Workshops usually combine multiple techniques within a single structured agenda.
How to run it: Pre-circulate context documents and an agenda. Open with objectives clarification. Use anonymous input first (sticky notes), then group discussion, then structured ranking. Close with agreed next steps and risk owners. See: Risk Workshop Facilitation Guide.
✓ Advantages
  • Most comprehensive technique — time and depth allow full exploration
  • Combines multiple approaches for maximum coverage
  • Produces an actionable, agreed output that participants own
  • Builds risk culture — education and identification happen simultaneously
✗ Disadvantages
  • Time-intensive — hard to schedule, especially across departments
  • Requires experienced facilitation to stay on track
  • Risk of "workshop fatigue" if done too frequently
  • Output quality drops significantly without pre-work from participants
Best for: Major projects, annual risk reviews, new business units, or post-incident reviews. Not suited to frequent use — save for high-impact occasions.
🎙
Expert Interviews
Individual Depth · Institutional Knowledge
One-on-one structured conversations with experienced team members, subject matter experts or senior leaders. The interviewer uses open-ended questions to surface knowledge and concerns that would never emerge in a group setting.
How to run it: Prepare a list of 5–8 open questions in advance (e.g. "What keeps you up at night about this project?" / "What's the one risk nobody talks about but everyone knows exists?"). Take notes carefully. Keep responses confidential unless agreed otherwise. Synthesise across all interviews afterward.
✓ Advantages
  • Captures institutional knowledge held by long-serving staff
  • People are more candid one-on-one than in groups
  • Surfaces risks that culture or hierarchy prevent from surfacing publicly
  • Builds trust and shows risk management takes everyone's input seriously
✗ Disadvantages
  • Time-consuming — each interview can take 30–60 minutes
  • Risk of interviewer bias influencing the responses
  • Limited to the knowledge and experience of the interviewee
  • Synthesis across multiple interviews requires skill and careful documentation
Best for: Strategic risk identification, succession planning risks, and uncovering hidden risks in legacy systems or long-established processes.
🚶
Process Walk-Through (FMEA)
Step-by-Step · Operational Focus
Also known as Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA), this technique involves systematically mapping each step of a business process and asking at every stage: "What could fail here? What would happen if it did?" It reveals single points of failure and hidden dependencies.
How to run it: Draw or map the process from start to finish. At each step, ask: What could go wrong? What is the cause? What is the impact? How likely is it? Can we detect it before it causes damage? Score each failure mode and prioritise remediation. See: ASQ FMEA Guide.
✓ Advantages
  • Highly systematic — very little gets missed when done thoroughly
  • Reveals single points of failure that other methods miss
  • Produces quantifiable output — risks can be scored and compared
  • Deeply familiar to regulated industries (healthcare, manufacturing, finance)
✗ Disadvantages
  • Time-intensive for complex processes with many steps
  • Requires detailed knowledge of the process being mapped
  • Can feel overly technical for non-specialist audiences
  • Less effective for identifying strategic or external risks
Best for: Operational risk identification in manufacturing, IT, healthcare, finance and any process-heavy environment. Always involve the people who actually perform the process — not just their managers.
✅
Risk Identification Checklists
Structured · Compliance-Ready
Pre-built checklists prompt the reviewer to consider a comprehensive list of potential risk areas relevant to their industry or function. Checklists can be developed internally from historical data or adapted from industry standards.
How to run it: Use or adapt an existing checklist relevant to your context (your Risk ID Checklist is included in your course materials). Work through each item systematically. Mark Yes (risk present), No (risk not applicable), or Unsure (needs further investigation). All "Yes" and "Unsure" items feed into the Risk Register.
✓ Advantages
  • Fast and accessible — anyone can use a checklist with minimal training
  • Ensures consistency across departments and over time
  • Particularly useful for regulated industries requiring documented evidence
  • Can be built from historical incidents to prevent recurrence
✗ Disadvantages
  • Only captures risks already known — new or emerging risks will be missed
  • Can create a false sense of completeness ("we ticked the boxes")
  • Checklists go stale — must be regularly updated to remain relevant
  • Does not promote creative thinking or peer discussion
Best for: Compliance-focused risk identification, onboarding new staff, and as a supplement to more creative techniques like workshops or group discussions.
📐 Best Practice

No single technique is sufficient on its own. The most effective risk identification programmes combine at least two or three approaches — for example, anonymous sticky notes to open up, followed by a structured group discussion, followed by a checklist to catch anything missed. Diversity of method = diversity of risks identified.

Chapter Four
Governance, Reporting & 3 Lines of Defense
Understanding who owns risk — and who checks it — is fundamental to making risk management work in any organisation.

4.1   What is Risk Governance?

Risk governance is the framework of structures, policies and accountabilities that an organisation uses to manage risk at every level. It defines who has authority to take risks, who monitors those risks, and who holds the organisation accountable.

Good risk governance answers three critical questions:

  • Who decides which risks to take and which to avoid?
  • Who monitors whether risk management is actually happening?
  • Who is accountable when something goes wrong?
📌 ISO 31000 on Governance

ISO 31000:2018 states that risk management should be integrated into all organisational activities — not treated as a separate function. This means governance of risk starts at Board level and flows through every layer of the organisation.

Reference: ISO 31000:2018 — Principle 2: Integrated

4.2   The 3 Lines of Defense Model

The 3 Lines of Defense (now formally known as the Three Lines Model per the IIA's 2020 update) is the most widely adopted framework for organising risk ownership, oversight and assurance in an organisation.

🏛   Board & Senior Management — Set Strategy · Define Risk Appetite · Govern All Three Lines
1st
Line
Business Operations
Own and manage the risks in their daily activities. Execute controls. Report issues promptly.
Sales · Operations · Finance · HR · IT · All front-line staff
2nd
Line
Risk & Compliance
Set the rules. Monitor 1st Line effectiveness. Advise and challenge on risk management.
Risk Management · Compliance · Legal · Information Security
3rd
Line
Internal Audit
Independently test whether Lines 1 and 2 are working. Report directly to the Board.
Internal Audit · Sometimes External Auditors
💡 Key Insight

The most important line is the 1st Line — because it is where risks are actually created and where controls are actually executed. The 2nd and 3rd Lines exist to support and verify the 1st Line, not to do its job for it. If the 1st Line is weak, no amount of oversight from the 2nd and 3rd Lines can compensate.

4.3   Risk Reporting & Escalation

Identifying a risk means nothing if it is not communicated to the right people at the right time. Risk reporting is the process of summarising, presenting and escalating risk information so that decision-makers can act.

Effective risk reporting follows three principles:

⏱
Timeliness
Risks must be reported quickly enough for the organisation to act. A risk reported after it has materialised into a loss is intelligence, not risk management.
🎯
Relevance
Reports should contain what decision-makers need — not everything that was identified. Boards need strategic-level information; operational managers need operational detail.
📊
Clarity
Risk information should be presented visually and simply. Heatmaps, dashboards and traffic-light ratings make risk data accessible to non-specialists.
🔄
Escalation Path
Every risk should have a clear escalation path — who to tell, in what format, and within what timeframe if the risk materialises or exceeds tolerance.
📌 Real-World Reference
IIA — The Three Lines Model (2020 Update)
In 2020 the Institute of Internal Auditors updated the classic "Three Lines of Defense" to the "Three Lines Model" — shifting emphasis from defense to value creation and collaboration. The update recognises that the three lines should actively work together, not function as separate silos.

Chapter Five
Case Study:
NovaTech Solutions
A corporate organisation faces a perfect storm of risk events across a single Monday morning. Apply everything from Chapters 1–4 to analyse what went wrong — and why.

5.1   Company Background

NovaTech Solutions is a mid-sized financial services firm with 1,200 employees across three regional offices. The company manages AED 2.5 billion in assets for 400 institutional and high-net-worth clients. It is regulated by the local financial authority and operates a core banking system, a CRM platform and an online client portal.

NovaTech has a Risk Management department (2nd Line), an Internal Audit function (3rd Line), and approximately 900 front-line staff in client-facing and operational roles (1st Line).

📘 Reading Instruction

As you read through the events below, for each event ask yourself: (1) What type of risk is this? (use the 6 categories from Chapter 2) and (2) Which Line of Defense should have caught or prevented it?

5.2   The Events Unfold

On a Monday in October, NovaTech experienced the following sequence of events:

TimeEventRisk Type
8:00 AMA phishing email reaches 40 staff inboxes. Three employees click the malicious link and enter their credentials. Attacker gains access to internal systems.Cyber Risk
9:30 AMThe client-facing portal goes offline. 400 clients cannot access their accounts or execute transactions. Helpdesk lines are overwhelmed.Operational Risk
10:15 AMCompliance receives an alert — client data may have been exfiltrated during the breach. GDPR notification obligations clock starts.Compliance Risk
11:00 AMA journalist contacts NovaTech's communications team — a client has leaked news of the outage on social media. Press enquiries begin.Reputational Risk
12:30 PMThe regulator calls, demanding an incident report within 24 hours. Internal Audit's six-month-old report flagging weak cyber controls is circulated.Compliance + Governance Failure
2:00 PMThree major clients call their relationship managers threatening to withdraw their portfolios. AED 180 million in assets at risk of departure.Financial Risk

5.3   Analysis & Lessons Learned

What makes the NovaTech case particularly instructive is that all three lines of defense failed simultaneously:

⚠ 1st Line Failure
Business Operations — The Front Line Did Not Hold
Three employees clicked a phishing link — indicating inadequate cybersecurity awareness training. The IT team had no real-time detection of the breach. The client portal had no failover system. These are all 1st Line controls that did not exist or were not working.
⚠ 2nd Line Failure
Risk & Compliance — Oversight Was Reactive, Not Proactive
The Compliance team was aware of GDPR obligations but had no real-time data monitoring. There was no documented cyber incident response plan. The Risk Management team had not translated their awareness of cyber threats into concrete controls for the 1st Line.
⚠ 3rd Line Failure
Internal Audit — The Warning Was Given and Ignored
Internal Audit had flagged inadequate cybersecurity controls in a report six months earlier. Management acknowledged the finding and accepted the risk — without implementing remediation. This is one of the most common governance failures in practice: identifying a risk and then doing nothing about it.
📐 The Deepest Lesson

Risk management is only as good as the culture that supports it. NovaTech had a Risk Management department, a Compliance function and an Internal Audit team. They had all three lines — on paper. But the Board did not act on the audit finding, the 2nd Line had no real enforcement mechanism, and the 1st Line had no awareness that cybersecurity was their responsibility too. Having the structure means nothing without the culture.

Human Factors
You can have the best risk framework in the world — and still fail. Because risk management ultimately depends on people. This chapter explains why.

6.1   What is Risk Culture?

Risk culture is the shared values, beliefs, attitudes and behaviours that shape how an organisation's people think about and respond to risk. It is the invisible force that determines whether risk management actually happens in practice — or only on paper.

Institute of Risk Management — Definition
"Risk culture is the norms of behaviour for individuals and groups within an organisation that determine the collective ability to identify, understand, openly discuss, escalate and act on the organisation's current and future risks."
Source: IRM — Risk Culture: Resources for Practitioners — theirm.org/risk-culture

In simple terms: if the framework is the skeleton of risk management, culture is the muscle. The skeleton defines the structure — but culture is what makes it move.

Risk culture is not something that can be designed in a policy document. It develops over time through leadership behaviour, how incidents are handled, whether people feel safe raising concerns, and whether risk is seen as a shared responsibility or someone else's problem.

💡 Key Insight

The NovaTech case study in Chapter 5 is ultimately a culture failure, not a process failure. The processes existed. The audit finding existed. The risk was known. But the culture did not support acting on it — and that is what caused the damage.

6.2   Why Culture Trumps Process

Organisations often invest heavily in risk frameworks, policies and tools — and then wonder why risks keep materialising. The answer, almost always, comes back to culture.

Consider the following scenario: a junior analyst notices something unusual in a client account that could indicate fraud. They have three choices:

😶
Stay Silent
In a blame-driven culture, raising concerns is risky for the person raising them. They fear looking incompetent, being dismissed, or damaging their career. So they say nothing.
🤔
Mention It Quietly
In a moderate culture, they might mention it informally to a colleague — but never formally escalate. The risk stays hidden and unmanaged.
✋
Escalate Immediately
In a strong risk culture, they raise it formally, knowing they will be listened to, supported and thanked for speaking up — regardless of the outcome.
📋
The Process Says...
All three organisations have the same escalation process in their policy document. The process didn't change the outcome. Culture did.
📌 Real-World Reference
Boeing 737 MAX — The Culture Behind the Crisis
Congressional investigations into the Boeing 737 MAX crashes (2018–2019, 346 deaths) found a culture where engineers who raised safety concerns were pressured to stay quiet, production schedules were prioritised over safety reporting, and management dismissed risk signals from its own employees. Two crashes and $20 billion in losses later, Boeing's culture — not just its processes — was identified as the root cause.

6.3   Human Factors in Risk

Research consistently shows that 73% of risk events involve human error as either a direct cause or a contributing factor (IBM, 2023). Yet most risk frameworks focus almost entirely on systems, processes and controls — and barely address the human beings operating them.

Human factors in risk fall into three broad categories:

Individual Factors

These are characteristics of the individual that affect risk behaviour — including skill, knowledge, fatigue, stress, personality and motivation.

FactorHow It Creates RiskExample
FatigueReduces attention, increases errors and slows response timeA finance team member processes payments late at night after a 14-hour day and makes a transfer error
OverconfidencePeople overestimate their own ability to manage a situationA senior trader takes an oversized position because "I've done this a hundred times"
Knowledge gapsStaff don't know what they don't know — and therefore don't flag risks they can't seeA new joiner doesn't escalate a GDPR concern because they don't recognise it as one
ComplacencyFamiliarity breeds inattention — routine tasks receive less care over timeA system administrator stops checking backup logs because "it's always been fine"

Team & Group Factors

Risk behaviour is shaped not just by individuals but by the groups they work in — and the social dynamics within those groups.

🐑
Groupthink
When teams prioritise harmony and agreement over honest assessment. Dissenting views are suppressed. Risks that "everyone knows about but nobody says" never get escalated. Classic in leadership teams and boards.
📢
Normalisation of Deviance
When repeated violations of procedure that "don't cause a problem" gradually become accepted as normal. Teams stop seeing them as risks. The NASA Challenger disaster was partly attributed to this — engineers had accepted O-ring erosion as "acceptable" over time.
🤐
Silence of the Lambs Effect
Junior staff who see risks clearly but lack the seniority or confidence to raise them formally. This is one of the most common sources of undiscovered organisational risk — and one of the best arguments for anonymous risk identification techniques.
🔄
Diffusion of Responsibility
When everyone assumes someone else is managing the risk. "That's the Risk Manager's job" — the myth we debunked in Chapter 1 — is actually a cultural manifestation of diffusion of responsibility.

Organisational Factors

Beyond individuals and teams, the organisation itself creates conditions that either encourage or suppress good risk behaviour:

  • Leadership behaviour — If leaders ignore risk frameworks themselves, staff will too. Culture flows downward. What the CEO tolerates becomes what the organisation accepts.
  • Incentive structures — If bonuses reward short-term results without regard for risk-taking, people will take risks to hit targets. The 2008 financial crisis was partly driven by bonus structures that rewarded volume over risk quality.
  • Communication channels — If there is no easy, safe way to report a risk concern, risks stay hidden. Anonymous hotlines, open-door policies and speak-up programmes are structural tools that support cultural change.
  • Response to incidents — How leadership handles the first escalated risk signal sets the tone for every future one. Blame leads to silence. Learning leads to openness.

6.4   Cognitive Biases That Create Risk

One of the most powerful — and most underappreciated — human factors in risk is cognitive bias. These are systematic errors in thinking that affect how people perceive, process and respond to risk information. They are not signs of stupidity. They are features of how the human brain works — and every professional is susceptible to them.

🧠
Optimism Bias
Most common bias in risk management
The tendency to overestimate the likelihood of positive events and underestimate the likelihood of negative ones. People consistently believe they are less likely to experience negative events than others around them.
In practice: "Our project won't have delays." "We've never had a data breach." "That won't happen to us." These are optimism bias speaking — not evidence-based risk assessment. Studies show 80% of drivers rate themselves as "above average" — statistically impossible.
How to counter it: Require evidence-based probability estimates. Use historical incident data rather than gut feeling. Run pre-mortems: "Assume this project has failed — why?"
⚓
Anchoring Bias
Distorts risk scoring and estimates
Over-reliance on the first piece of information received when making a decision. Once an anchor is set, subsequent judgements are heavily influenced by it — even when the anchor is arbitrary or incorrect.
In practice: If a risk is initially scored as "low" in a register, it tends to stay low — even when new evidence suggests it should be higher. The initial score becomes the anchor. Teams spend more time defending the original estimate than updating it.
How to counter it: Regularly challenge existing risk scores with fresh eyes. Bring in people who weren't involved in the original assessment to review entries.
📰
Availability Bias
Skews likelihood estimates toward recent events
The tendency to judge the probability of an event based on how easily examples come to mind — usually driven by recency, vividness or media coverage. Events we can remember easily feel more likely than they actually are.
In practice: After a major data breach in the news, organisations suddenly prioritise cyber risk — regardless of their actual exposure. After a quiet period with no incidents, the same risks feel less urgent. The risk hasn't changed — only our recent experience of it has.
How to counter it: Base risk assessments on data and statistics, not recent headlines. Maintain consistent review schedules regardless of recent incidents in the news.
🙈
Normalcy Bias
Underestimation of catastrophic events
The tendency to underestimate the possibility of a severe disruption and its potential impact — because it has never happened before. People assume that because a disaster hasn't occurred, it won't occur.
In practice: "We've been operating for 20 years with no major incident." This is not evidence that the risk is low — it may just mean you've been fortunate. Normalcy bias is particularly dangerous for low-frequency, high-impact risks like pandemics, cyberattacks on critical infrastructure and financial system shocks.
How to counter it: Use scenario planning and stress testing. Ask: "What would happen if our least likely high-impact risk materialised tomorrow?" Make the hypothetical feel concrete.

6.5   Building a Healthy Risk Culture

Risk culture cannot be mandated with a policy. It is built gradually — through consistent leadership behaviour, structural enablers and genuine commitment to learning. Here is what it looks like in practice:

Cultural EnablerWhat It Looks Like in PracticeRed Flag (Opposite)
Psychological SafetyPeople raise concerns without fear of blame or career consequences. Leaders thank staff for escalating bad news."Don't bring me problems, bring me solutions." Concerns are dismissed or punished.
Leadership Role-ModellingSenior leaders visibly follow risk processes, attend risk training, and openly discuss their own uncertainty.Leaders bypass controls "because they're senior." Risk is seen as a junior concern.
Learning from IncidentsEvery incident triggers a no-blame root cause analysis. Findings are shared across the organisation.Incidents are hushed up or blamed on individuals. The same mistakes recur.
Clear Risk OwnershipEvery identified risk has a named owner who is accountable and regularly reviews their risks.Risks are "owned" by the Risk Manager — meaning nobody owns them.
Regular Risk ConversationsRisk is a standing agenda item in team meetings — not just an annual exercise.Risk register last updated 11 months ago. Nobody knows where it is.
Speak-Up MechanismsAnonymous reporting channels, open-door policies and whistleblower protection are actively promoted and used.The whistleblower hotline exists on paper. Nobody trusts it or uses it.
🔑 What Good Looks Like

In organisations with strong risk cultures, risk management is not a compliance exercise that happens in a separate department. It is how decisions are made everywhere, every day. It is the question a manager asks before approving a project. It is the conversation a team has when a near-miss occurs. It is the instinct a new joiner develops in their first six months — because the culture around them demonstrates it constantly.

📌 Real-World Reference
Google — Project Aristotle and Psychological Safety
In 2012, Google launched Project Aristotle — a study of what made some teams perform significantly better than others. After analysing 180 teams, the single biggest factor was not skill, experience or strategy. It was psychological safety — the belief that team members could take risks, raise concerns and admit mistakes without being punished. High-performing teams had cultures where speaking up was encouraged and valued.

This finding has profound implications for risk management: the teams best at managing risk are not those with the most elaborate frameworks — they are the ones where people feel safe enough to say "I'm worried about this."

📌 What You Can Do — Starting Now

You don't need to be in a leadership position to build risk culture. As a new professional, you can contribute immediately by:

  • Asking "what could go wrong?" before starting new tasks or projects
  • Raising concerns early — even when it feels uncomfortable
  • Sharing near-misses with your team so others can learn
  • Treating risk as part of your role — not someone else's job
  • Recognising your own cognitive biases — especially optimism bias
